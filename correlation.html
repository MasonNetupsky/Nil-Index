Spend vs. Outcomes

DOES SPENDING
ACTUALLY WIN GAMES?

Every NIL dollar is a bet that money buys wins. We ran the actual numbers — team NIL estimate against win percentage — for every school in the dataset with a reported figure, sport by sport. The relationship is real, but it's weaker than the arms race narrative suggests.

NIL Estimate vs. Win %
Football
25 programs with reported NIL estimates, 2024 season.
r = 0.60
Moderate-to-Strong Positive
R² = 0.36 — NIL spend explains roughly 36% of the variance in win rate across these programs.

Football shows the clearest relationship of the four sports outside volleyball. Texas, Miami, Texas A&M, and Oregon all sit near the top of both axes. But the exceptions matter: LSU spent $38M and won just 53.8% of its games, while Arkansas' $22M budget produced a 16.7% win rate — among the worst in the sample. Money buys talent, not scheme fit or health, and both of those still decide close games.

NIL Estimate vs. Win %
Men's Basketball
40 programs with reported NIL estimates, 2024–25 season.
r = 0.45
Moderate Positive
R² = 0.20 — the weakest relationship of the four sports. NIL spend explains only about 20% of the variance in win rate.

Kentucky's $22M collective is roughly double the next-highest budget, yet Duke and Michigan won at 92%+ clips on budgets closer to $10–12M. Basketball rosters are smaller and rotations tighter, so a well-coached team can out-execute a bigger spender far more easily than in football. Depth and matchups still carry more weight than payroll size in March.

R² = 0.27 — NIL spend explains about 27% of the variance in win rate.

Georgia posted a 79.1% win rate on a $900K collective, outperforming several programs spending over $1M. Baseball's NIL market is still emerging and draft eligibility complicates who collectives can even commit to long-term, which likely keeps development and coaching more decisive here than in the two revenue sports.

Women's Volleyball

r = 0.83
Strong Positive
R² = 0.69 — the strongest relationship in the dataset. NIL spend explains roughly 69% of the variance in win rate.

Nebraska's $1.8M collective and 97.1% win rate anchor the top of both axes, and the pattern holds fairly consistently down the list. Smaller rosters and lower per-athlete costs likely mean volleyball NIL dollars go further toward locking in a program's best players — making budget a much stronger predictor here than in any other sport tracked.
